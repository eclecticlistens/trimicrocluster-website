Dyslexia, AI & creative process

Thirty years of ideas.
A different way to work.

The ambition to make this kind of music came long before AI. What changed was my ability to turn an idea into something I could build, hear and develop.

A personal account by Nick, written collaboratively from the development conversations.

The barrier was getting it down

For more than thirty years, I have wanted to make music and instruments that develop in the ways I imagine. Having the idea has never been the whole problem. Getting it out of my head, organising it on paper and keeping enough of a record to continue has often been the difficult part.

As someone with dyslexia, I find that process frustrating. I can know what I want to hear and still struggle to explain it in a conventional written form. The effort of documenting a project can take me away from the creative work that made me want to start it.

“It’s given focused steps. Allowed me to just input chat about ideas”

Nick, reflecting on the TMC working process, 23 October 2026.

Working through conversation has made a real difference. I can start with an impression, a musical relationship or something that feels wrong in a recording. I do not have to arrive with a finished technical specification. The discussion helps me find the next useful thing to try.

Starting with conversation

That does not make the work effortless. It changes where the effort goes. Instead of spending all my attention turning a thought into formal documentation, I can describe it, question the interpretation and hear a practical attempt. The writing develops alongside the instrument rather than becoming a barrier in front of it.

The conversations also leave a history. We can return to an earlier question, see why a version was rejected and recover the intention behind a feature. Important conversations and accepted versions still need to be saved independently: a useful working memory is not the same thing as a complete archive.

How an idea becomes a test

TMC has developed through repeated cycles of talking, trying and listening. Some ideas become useful features. Others are simplified, postponed or dropped. Keeping those outcomes visible explains the instrument better than a story in which every suggestion succeeds.

A decision made by listening

The Objects percussion work offers a concrete example. Its purpose was not simply to add more drum activity. I wanted separate events and motifs to find changing relationships without taking over the rest of the music.

Earlier Pulse and Fracture approaches were set aside as development concentrated on Objects. During the 20 October session, Pad 8's Slicer behaviour was protected while attention moved to Pads 1–6. Pad 7 was left unchanged for later manual exploration. Those boundaries mattered: an improvement in one part should not quietly undo a result I already liked.

Before the accepted take

There were still corrections to make to participation and the relationships between the percussion parts. A working proposal was not yet an accepted musical result.

After listening

Following a listening session of about twenty minutes, I accepted the result and asked for that version to be kept. The backup marked a point worth returning to.

This is why I want before-and-after examples in the archive. They can show the conversation, the attempted change and the musical judgement together. Where the corresponding recordings are available, readers should be able to hear the distinction rather than take the description on trust.

Authorship and assistance

AI is involved in more than typing code. It can suggest ideas, explain unfamiliar material, organise research and help turn a complicated conversation into a sequence of practical steps. Those suggestions can be useful without being right for TMC.

My role is to decide what the instrument is trying to do, what it should sound like and what survives the test of listening. I do not need to claim that I wrote every line myself to take responsibility for the musical decisions. Equally, the assistance and the work of the original software authors should be acknowledged rather than hidden.

AI helps me make and communicate the work. It does not remove the need to question it.

Code can fail. An explanation can sound more certain than its evidence allows. A promising technique can be musically unsuitable. Discussing those limits is part of documenting the collaboration, not a reason to conceal it.

More than one way to read

This website is being built around the same practical aim: the writing should help someone reach the ideas, not make the ideas harder to reach. Diagrams explain relationships, shorter passages introduce the concepts, and optional technical detail provides a deeper route.

The Reading layout button offers larger text and more space. The diagrams are accompanied by ordinary text, and future listening studies will add another way to understand what is happening. These are choices readers can use, not a prescription for a single correct way to read.

My experience does not stand for everyone with dyslexia. It may still be useful to someone who recognises the frustration of having an idea but finding the conventional route to expressing it difficult.

About this account and its sources

The first-person prose is an AI-assisted editorial draft based on Nick's account of 23 October 2026 and the development conversations. Only the marked quotation is presented as a direct excerpt. The Objects example draws on the accepted 20 October listening session; it does not claim that the whole Advanced system was finished.
