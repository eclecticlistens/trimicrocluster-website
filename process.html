Dyslexia, AI & Creative Process

The personal and practical story of how conversational AI helped bridge long-standing barriers between musical ideas, written documentation and working instrument development.

In brief

The musical ideas did not originate from a language model. AI helped turn conversation into documented plans, programming tests, structured research and a retrievable historical record. Artistic judgement remained central.

Thirty years of ideas

First-person draft for artist review

For more than thirty years, I have had musical ideas that I struggled to realise in the ways I imagined. The difficulty wasn't necessarily having ideas, but turning them into something structured and documented that I could return to. As someone with dyslexia, conventional written documentation often made that process difficult and frustrating.

With conversational AI, I can begin by talking about what I want to hear. An informal idea can become a series of manageable steps: a discussion, a design, an experiment, and then a listening test. Just as importantly, there is a record of the conversation that led to the result.

I want the process documented openly because it may help other creative people who find traditional written methods a barrier. This is not a claim that AI replaces musical judgement or that its output is always correct. It is an account of how assistance has changed what I can practically attempt.

The real collaboration loop

The loop matters because many promising ideas do not work in practice. The conversation is a starting point, but the listening test supplies evidence. Earlier concepts can be rejected or revisited without the history being lost.

Before and after: Objects

12 Oct 2026

Before

Pulse and Fracture had been explored as percussion profiles. The artist chose to abandon those strands and concentrate on an eight-pad Objects ecosystem.

After

A tested Objects baseline was accepted after corrections to Pad 8 participation and evolving Pads 1–6 ensemble relationships. The accepted recording/listening experience, not just generated code, was decisive.

What each participant contributes

Human authorship

Musical ambition, aesthetic decisions, knowledge of the hardware, listening, rejecting unsuitable approaches, and deciding what to share.

AI-assisted work

Organising discussions, explaining unfamiliar technical ideas, proposing alternatives, assisting with programming and tests, searching references, and drafting the historical archive.

Making the website readable

The content is divided into short sections with a summary at the top, descriptive headings and explanatory images. Each page includes an optional Reading layout control, which enlarges body type, increases spacing and narrows the reading column.

Readers have different needs and preferences. This site deliberately avoids claiming that one special font or one background colour is universally best for dyslexia. The W3C text-spacing guidance supports flexible personal settings.

Limitations and honesty

AI-generated code can fail; generated explanations can overstate certainty; chat histories and memory summaries may omit details. For that reason, a trustworthy archive needs versioned code, dated decisions, test results, recordings and clear correction notes, not just a polished retrospective.
