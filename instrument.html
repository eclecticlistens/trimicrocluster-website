Sound · Relationships · Listening

An instrument that leaves room for change.

TriMicroCluster brings sustained tones, phrases and short events into a shared musical setting. The interest lies in what happens between them, not simply in how many notes they produce.

A way of making music

TMC is a generative instrument developed for monome norns. It is not one finished composition: it establishes conditions in which musical material can develop. Its maker chooses the direction, listens to the results and changes those conditions when the music calls for it.

The project centres on microtonal pitch relationships, different kinds of movement and enough independence for the parts to surprise one another. A held tone can make a brief event sound different. A returning phrase can become familiar without the whole passage becoming a fixed loop.

That balance is the point of the instrument. Autonomy does not mean accepting whatever happens. It means creating a system whose behaviour is worth listening to, then exercising judgement about what should remain.

Three related questions

The early architecture separated AOS, concerned with pitch and gravitational relationships, from TRI, concerned with how a layer behaves. A shared Conductor addresses another question: how applicable parts participate over time.

Anchor, Orbit and Shimmer describe related roles: reference, movement and variation.

How does it develop?

The TRI vocabulary describes behavioural approaches rather than a different sound source for every state.

When may it join?

The Conductor supplies temporal conditions for applicable layers, rather than writing every event.

Does it work musically?

The answer comes from listening. A convincing explanation is not enough to make a behaviour worth keeping.

Meet the sound layers

The October 2026 Advanced arrangement has eight principal sound layers. They do not all play the same way. Some sustain a pitch setting, some form phrases, and others contribute shorter activity.

The map is a way into listening, not a suggestion that every part should always be present. Hearing two or three layers together can reveal their relationship more clearly than turning everything on.

What came before

TMC began with Bloom-related pitch material and cellular ideas. Developing a separate identity involved deciding which parts to change, not pretending the earlier work had never existed.

The change from Kal/Bell sounds towards Rings Cells illustrates that process. Nick wanted a different sound while preserving useful aspects of cell behaviour and memory. An inherited organisation could be reconsidered independently of the sound that first made it audible. Specific later memory controls were explored as ideas; they should not all be assumed to exist in the current instrument.

The name TriMicroCluster brings these concerns together: a three-way relationship, a microtonal pitch world and musical material heard in interacting groups.

Listening is part of the instrument

TMC can continue without a performer specifying every note, but its development is full of human choices. Nick listens for activity that becomes too insistent, relationships that stop developing and changes that distract from the whole. Keeping a version is a musical decision as well as a technical one.

The listening studies will make those relationships easier to hear. The creative-process account explains how conversation and AI assistance have made it possible to turn them into a working instrument and a written record.
