Curtis Roads — Microsound

Discussed in development

Microsound considers composition at the scale of sound particles and the changing perceptions of points, pulses, tones and textures. It is a useful place to think about how individual events can become a larger musical surface as their relationships change.

Nick already owned the book when it entered these conversations. It was part of the earlier synthesis research and returned explicitly in the 14 October discussion about TMC's percussion. The useful question is not “Which features can we borrow?” but “What does this help us hear or describe more clearly?”

Reading and implementation are separate. Pulsar synthesis was considered and later dropped from the TMC direction on 2 September because it did not fit the intended musical result. The presence of that topic in the book is not evidence that TMC uses it.

The platform beneath the instrument

monome — norns documentation

The official documentation describes the division between Lua scripts, which organise controls and behaviour, and SuperCollider engines, which generate and process sound. It helps explain how an instrument can develop within norns without requiring its maker to build the whole underlying platform.

Musical context is not an algorithm source

Autechre and Gerald Donald were explicitly added to the research interests in September. The interest was in sound creation, drum programming and the use of systems: how musical character can arise from relationships, restrictions and changing processes.

That context should remain recognisable without turning it into an unsupported technical lineage. An interest in an artist does not show how their private system works, nor prove that TMC implements a method used by them. Specific interviews or first-hand explanations can be added when the particular source has been checked.

Related research, not a feature list

The wider research into interactive instruments and human–machine collaboration is also a source of personal learning. It is not automatically a TMC development agenda. The papers below are suggested comparisons for readers, rather than claims about where the Conductor or AOS originated.

Further reading

“Instant Design”

Hugh Aynsley, Pete Bennett, Dave Meckin, Sven Hollowell and Thomas J. Mitchell examine five approaches to using generative text-to-image tools in workshops for new musical instrument concepts. It offers a comparison with iterative design and clearly framed questions, while differing from TMC's conversational programming process.

NIME 2025, pp. 26–32. Proceedings record and abstract ↗

Further reading

Touching Wires

Sandy Ma and Charles Patrick Martin explore a textile interface for human–AI musical co-creation. Its relevance here is the relationship between a person and a system, including the need to understand the AI's role. It is not an account of TMC's hardware, synthesis or timing logic.

NIME 2025, pp. 296–303. Proceedings record and abstract ↗

Reading should not become another obstacle

The website's presentation draws on W3C guidance recommending clear language, short blocks of text, useful images and visual separation. Those principles support the intention to offer more than one route into an idea: an article, an explanatory diagram and, when recordings are available, a listening example.

They do not imply that every dyslexic reader wants the same font or layout. The optional reading presentation is a choice, and the diagrams remain accompanied by text.

The conversations are part of the source material

TMC's history is spread across discussions of architecture, research, music, samples, routing and performance. They preserve different kinds of information: an early intention, a practical obstacle, a discarded experiment or an accepted listening result.

The articles bring those threads together rather than treating one summary as the whole history. Later decisions take precedence when the direction changes, while earlier choices remain useful for explaining how the instrument arrived there.

Reading a paper, discussing an idea, implementing a change and accepting its musical result are distinct events. Keeping that distinction visible allows the writing to be clear without inventing a neat origin story for every feature.
